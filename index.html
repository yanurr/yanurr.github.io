SOVEREIGNTY
Popularization of a master thesis

Can you be sovereign on software you don't run?

A former air force general wins Bulgaria's election in a landslide. He takes personal control of defence, intelligence, and national security. He has called for restoring ties with Moscow. His military runs on Microsoft.

This is a story about who actually controls Europe's most critical infrastructure and why Brussels keeps calling it a supply-chain problem instead of a political one.

I — The dependency

Europe outsourced its digital backbone. Now it's trying to figure out who can flip the switch.

Over the past two decades, European governments, militaries, and public institutions quietly migrated their most sensitive operations onto infrastructure owned by a handful of US companies. It happened gradually: a cloud contract here, a productivity suite there, and for a long time nobody talked about it as a political problem. It was a procurement decision. Then the world changed.

These figures describe a power relationship. Under the US CLOUD Act of 2018, American companies are legally required to hand over user data on government request, regardless of where that data is physically stored. If a European defence ministry's logistics system runs on Azure, Washington can compel access to it. Brussels cannot prevent that.

Under oath — July 2025
Microsoft France's legal director, Anton Carniaux, testified before the French Senate that he could not guarantee French government data was safe from silent US access requests, even when stored on servers physically located in the EU.
Source: IT Daily; Heise Online, 2025

It's not just cloud. In payments, Visa and Mastercard process roughly two-thirds of eurozone card transactions. 13 of 19 eurozone countries have no meaningful domestic alternative, resulting in over 96% of card payments flowing through US networks.

II — Two countries, two logics

Same dependency. Opposite conclusions.

What a government does about digital dependency depends on who it sees as the threat. These two cases sit at opposite ends of the spectrum.

Austria
Decoupling by design

Constitutionally neutral. Outside NATO, but embedded in EU defence cooperation. Its threat perception doesn't centre on Russia the way Eastern flank states' does. A case for US dependency as a security asset is correspondingly weaker.

16,000
military workstations migrated from Microsoft Office to LibreOffice by end of 2025, one of Europe's largest open-source deployments

"We are not doing this to save money. We are doing this so that the Armed Forces — an organisation which is there to function when everything else is down — can continue to have products that work within our sphere of influence."

The military contributed over five person-years of code back to the LibreOffice project. It runs its own Linux and Samba infrastructure, never having used Microsoft's cloud or email services in the first place.

Sources: Heise Online, Sep 2025; Janes Defence, Oct 2025; TDF Blog, Sep 2025

Bulgaria
When alignment becomes the vulnerability

NATO member state on Europe's eastern flank. Documented target of Russian hybrid operations: cyberattacks, disinformation, alleged intelligence-linked assassination attempts. For years, reliance on US cloud providers was treated not as dependency but as strategic alignment.

44.7%
vote share won by Radev's Progressive Bulgaria in April 2026 — the largest single-party result since 1997, ending eight successive elections

Then the logic flipped. Rumen Radev — a former president who called for resuming Russian energy imports and described Bulgaria as a mediator between the EU and Moscow — won a landslide. He assumed personal control of Defence, Interior, Intelligence, and the National Agency for State Security.

The security argument that justified the dependency is now overseen by a leader who has publicly questioned the alliance underpinning it.

Sources: Al Jazeera, Apr 2026; ECFR, Apr–May 2026; Novinite, May 2026; CSD, 2026

National security logics are not fixed constants. They are sites of political contestation and what looks like a stable foundation for infrastructure policy can be reorganised in a single election.

III — What Brussels says (and doesn't)

Five years of sovereignty talk. Where are we?

The EU's response has accelerated but watch the language. "Sovereignty" is avoided precisely where it would name an adversary. The Commission prefers "de-risking" and "diversification": terms that frame digital dependency as a supply-chain management problem, not a political control problem.

2019–2020
Rhetoric
GAIA-X: the "Airbus of the cloud"
Franco-German initiative to build a European cloud ecosystem. Ambitious pitch but it welcomed the very hyperscalers it was supposed to counter as members. By 2024, Nextcloud's CEO called it a "paper monster." Forrester concluded it had "failed to launch meaningful services."
Forrester Research; EuroStack Directory; InfoWorld
2024
Blocked
EUCS sovereignty requirement stripped
Seven Member States (Denmark, Estonia, Greece, Ireland, Netherlands, Poland, Sweden) co-signed a non-paper opposing sovereignty requirements in EU cloud certification. Their argument: excluding US hyperscalers would weaken European security. The Belgian presidency compromise removed the requirement.
EUISS, Nov 2025; ENISA; European DIGITAL SME Alliance
December 2025
Rhetoric
Joint Communication on Economic Security
The Commission's most comprehensive statement on digital dependency. Uses "de-risking" and "diversification" throughout and carefully avoids "sovereignty." This thesis argues the choice is a political act: "diversification" implies a market governance problem; "sovereignty" implies a political control problem that names an adversary.
JOIN(2025) 977 final
January 2026
Action
Parliament votes, France acts
Cross-party majority (EPP, S&D, Liberals, Greens) adopts a report calling for reduced US tech reliance. Same week, France announces all government departments will replace Microsoft Teams and Zoom with Visio, a nationally-hosted, open-source platform on SecNumCloud-certified infrastructure, by 2027.
Heise Online; Euronews; Help Net Security, Jan 2026
June 2026
Action
European Technological Sovereignty Package
The Commission's most ambitious move yet. The Cloud and AI Development Act (CADA) introduces a four-tier sovereignty assurance framework for cloud services. Accompanied by the Chips Act 2.0 and a new EU Open-Source Strategy. For the first time, the Commission puts "sovereignty" in the title.
European Commission, 3 Jun 2026; Digital Watch Observatory; Bird & Bird

Notice the pattern. The Member States most exposed to Russian hybrid threats are the ones most resistant to excluding US providers. The fragmentation is not a failure of coordination. It reflects genuinely incompatible threat perceptions within the Union and that is the thesis's central finding.

IV — The argument

Sovereignty cannot be installed like software

The EU's current approach of certifying cloud providers, encouraging diversification, and nudging procurement rules manages risk without confronting the underlying power asymmetry. It accommodates rather than disrupts.

Austria shows that decoupling is possible, within limits. A neutral state with institutional capacity and political will can migrate its military off Microsoft and run its own infrastructure. But it replaced an office suite, not the entire technology stack. The structural conditions of global digital capitalism constrain how far any single state can go.

Bulgaria shows something more unsettling: that the security logic justifying dependency is itself political, not structural. The same NATO alignment that made US cloud dependency a security asset in 2024 is now presided over by a government whose leader has publicly questioned that framework.

If the argument for dependency rests on alliance, what happens when the alliance becomes contested?

EU digital sovereignty is not a uniform project. It is mediated by differing national contexts, competing threat perceptions, and structural conditions that no single policy instrument can resolve. The fragmentation across Member States is not an obstacle to sovereignty. It is what sovereignty, in the EU's political reality, actually looks like.
